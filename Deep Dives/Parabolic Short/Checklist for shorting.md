### Prerequisite: It must be an "in-play" stock

Before any variable applies, the stock needs to be moving on unusually high volume and trading in a wider-than-average range, usually triggered by news or a technical break. Without large ranges and heavy emotion/price discovery, there's no edge to exploit.

### The 10 Variables (mental checklist, rated collectively — not pass/fail individually)

**1. Acceleration / rate of change, not distance traveled**  
It's not how far a stock has moved, but whether the move is accelerating (parabolic/asymptotic) rather than linear. A steady, linear slope — even a big cumulative move (like silver's slow climb) — is _not_ a signal. Only true parabolic acceleration near the end of a move qualifies. Interpretation: don't fade a move just because it's "up a lot"; fade it only when the slope itself is curving sharply.

**2. Multiple days in the same direction**  
Ideally 3+ consecutive days in one direction; more is better, especially combined with acceleration. Choppy up-down-up-down action doesn't count, even if the stock has technically declined overall. This is a spectrum, not a hard cutoff — two strong days with other variables aligned can still qualify.

**3. Extreme extension from Bollinger Bands**  
Measure distance from the 20-period moving average (2 std devs) in both $ and %. The bigger the extension, the bigger the theoretical "reward" if it reverts to the mean (the average = equilibrium = potential profit target). A stock barely poking above/below the band isn't extended enough — most losses ("paper cuts") come from fading moves that aren't actually extreme yet.

**4. Avoid fading fresh news**  
Big rule: don't mean-revert something on fresh, fundamental news (earnings shock, supply disruption, etc.) — because news can represent a genuine repricing, not just emotional overshoot. Wait for price action _days after_ the news, once it's pure sentiment/technicals driving further movement, not the news itself. Judging "how stale is the news" is subjective and is the gray area to be careful with (his oil vs. silver example).

**5. Huge increase in volume (multiples of average)**  
Volume tells the real psychological story — massive volume resets the average cost basis of holders, changing the crowd's psychology (from underwater to breakeven/in-the-money), which fuels a bounce. Look for volume that dwarfs surrounding bars, and compare intraday volume (e.g., 9:30–10:30) to the same window on prior already-elevated days.

**6. Multiple legs in the same direction**  
More sequential "legs" of the move = more people chasing late = bigger eventual capitulation. The reward on a reversal isn't just the last leg — it can be a retrace of the _entire_ multi-leg move back to the average. First legs out of a range are less interesting; the 2nd/3rd/final legs are where capitulation truly happens.

**7. Minimal consolidation during the move**  
Consolidation = "price acceptance" = the market agreeing on a price = a poor setup for reversion. Want to see minimal sideways chop and mostly directional bars with little pause — pauses reduce the odds of an outsized reversal.

**8. Extreme sentiment (narrative extremes)**  
Watch for language like "uninvestable," "new paradigm," "can't afford to not have exposure," "cheap at any price." These verbal extremes (Twitter/CNBC, etc.) mark emotional peaks. Key interpretive test: check whether the underlying fundamental (e.g., the dollar index during the "dollar debasement" silver narrative) actually moved as much as the narrative claims — if not, it's pure sentiment, which is unsustainable and reversion-prone.

**9. The more "boring" the underlying, the better**  
Large-cap, diversified, stable businesses (Berkshire, Apple, boring metals) are far more likely to have their true fundamental value unchanged despite a panic — so a big move is more likely pure sentiment/narrative and thus more likely to revert. Small/sketchy/single-product names (micro-cap biotech) are far less trustworthy to mean-revert, because a big drop there might reflect a real fundamental impairment.

**10. Market structure/forced flows magnifying the move**  
Look for signs of margin calls, forced liquidations, or forced buying/selling that's divorced from fundamentals (e.g., a buyer repeatedly defending an obviously "wrong" price because they're forced to cover, not because they want to buy). When you can see this footprint (unnatural buying/selling that persists until it abruptly stops), it signals the move is structural, not fundamental — and likely to snap back hard once the forced flow ends.
